% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\)
\abstract{
Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) play an important role in protecting network security. But, a major bottleneck is selecting optimal features from high-dimensional traffic data which affects both classification efficiency and computational overhead. We propose an enhanced bio-inspired feature selection approach based on a modified Student Psychology-Based Optimization (SPBO) algorithm integrated with an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy and a Lévy flight mechanism to overcome this challenge. The $\epsilon$-greedy policy dynamically balances exploration and exploitation, while Lévy flight supplies the heavy-tailed jumps that allow search agents to escape local optima. The selected subsets were evaluated with multiple machine learning classifiers on the NSL-KDD dataset for multi-level classification. The proposed $\epsilon$-L-SPBO reduced the feature space from 41 attributes to 10, discarding approximately 76\% of the attributes, and converged within 7 iterations. Combined with a Random Forest classifier, the 10-feature subset achieved a classification accuracy of 99.91\%, outperforming several state-of-the-art metaheuristic feature selection methods evaluated under an identical protocol while requiring less runtime. Model interpretability was established using Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques, specifically SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME), providing transparency into feature contributions. Overall, the framework offers an accurate, computationally efficient, and interpretable solution for modern network intrusion detection.
}

\section{Introduction}

Ensuring robust cybersecurity has become increasingly essential for the secure and reliable advancement of the Fourth Industrial Revolution (Industry 4.0). Security threats to network infrastructures have become an alarming issue worldwide across all environments. Every year, billions of dollars in economic loss and vast amounts of private information are compromised due to security-related breaches. Furthermore, the exponential proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, cloud computing, and next-generation communication networks has drastically expanded the attack surface, rendering traditional static defenses insufficient \cite{b22}. In recent years, several high-profile security incidents that occurred throughout the world have made us acutely aware of the importance of developing adaptive, intelligent network security mechanisms.

An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) is a vital software application or hardware device intended to monitor the activities of network traffic to identify malicious content or anomalous patterns. It is designed in such a way that it emits alerts and reports upon discovery of malicious content. Although the majority of IDS systems are designed passively to detect and report suspicious network activity, it should be noted that there are advanced Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) that have the capability to actively block suspicious network traffic \cite{b4}.

However, modern IDSs deal with a massive amount of data in daily network traffic. These datasets inherently contain high-dimensional, redundant, noisy, and irrelevant features, which severely affect the IDS’s predictive performance and consume excessive computational resources. This phenomenon, often referred to as the "curse of dimensionality," can cause machine learning classifiers to overfit and delay real-time threat detection \cite{b24}. For that reason, dimension reduction is needed to enhance the IDS’s efficiency, reduce execution time, and improve other performance metrics \cite{b5}.

Feature selection is a fundamental Machine Learning (ML) preprocessing concept utilized to overcome these data challenges. It is the process of selecting the most relevant features that contribute to building a robust model \cite{b19}. Feature selection can be done manually or using several automated techniques and algorithms, such as statistical analysis, machine learning, and neural networks. The selection mechanism is used to select the relevant features in a dataset \cite{b6}. It is a mandatory step in building a robust IDS to eliminate irrelevant features that raise false alarms, thereby increasing the accuracy and reliability of the desired system.

While traditional feature selection techniques—such as filter and wrapper methods—are common, they frequently suffer from high computational complexity or tend to stagnate in local optima when evaluating massive feature spaces \cite{b25}. Because selecting the optimal feature subset from high-dimensional data is an NP-hard problem, metaheuristic algorithms are increasingly used for feature selection in IDS to achieve a higher level of detection accuracy and computational efficiency \cite{b26}.

Bio-inspired optimization algorithms are an important sub-category used in the implementation of metaheuristics. Bio-inspired refers to a collective and artificial intelligence that is mathematically modeled after the evolutionary processes or social behaviors of swarms, animals, or human psychology. These algorithms are specifically designed to solve complex, non-linear problems by efficiently navigating vast search spaces to find near-optimal feature subsets where traditional exact methods fail \cite{b27}.

Despite this progress, the metaheuristic feature selection literature for IDS leaves three questions open. The prevailing update rules draw every candidate towards an incumbent reference solution, so population diversity decays as the search converges and the algorithm stagnates once that incumbent stops improving. Exploration is governed implicitly, through fixed operator probabilities rather than through a policy that reacts to the progress of the search, so the computational budget is consumed uniformly whether or not the population is still discovering useful subsets. Finally, the security relevance of a selected subset is seldom examined; selected attributes are reported as a list of indices and their contribution to distinguishing attack classes is assumed rather than demonstrated.

This paper addresses those three questions with $\epsilon$-L-SPBO, a modified Student Psychology-Based Optimization algorithm for intrusion detection feature selection. SPBO is adopted because its variable-dependent categorisation already sustains more diversity than a single global update rule, and it is extended with an $\epsilon$-greedy policy and a Lévy flight mechanism that address the exploration and stagnation problems directly. The algorithm is evaluated on the NSL-KDD benchmark across multiple machine learning classifiers, and the selected subset is examined with explainable AI. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item An enhanced SPBO variant, $\epsilon$-L-SPBO, is proposed, in which an $\epsilon$-greedy policy replaces the implicit exploration of the standard algorithm with an explicit and tunable probabilistic decision, while a Lévy flight mechanism supplies the heavy-tailed jumps that allow search agents to leave local optima rather than oscillate around them.
    \item The proposed algorithm is evaluated on the NSL-KDD benchmark, where it reduces the feature space from 41 attributes to 10, discarding approximately 76\% of the attribute space, and converges within 7 iterations.
    \item Five machine learning classifiers are trained on the selected subset under a common experimental protocol, with the Random Forest classifier attaining 99.91\% accuracy, and the results are compared against several state-of-the-art metaheuristic feature selection algorithms re-run under identical conditions.
    \item The operational relevance of the retained attributes is verified using SHAP and LIME, so that their contribution to distinguishing normal traffic from attack classes is demonstrated rather than assumed.
    \item A structured review of metaheuristic feature selection for intrusion detection is provided, organised by algorithm family and summarised in a comparative table.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: state-of-the-art works are presented in Section~\ref{rw}. Section~\ref{spbo} introduces the Student Psychology Based Optimization technique. The proposed feature algorithm $\epsilon$-L-SPBO is discussed in detail in Section~\ref{ms}, while Section~\ref{eara} discusses the experimental results. Section~\ref{abl} reports the ablation studies, Section~\ref{di} presents the discussion of the overall results, and Section~\ref{co} concludes the paper. The problem setting addressed in this work is summarised in Figure~\ref{f2}.

\section{Related Works}
\label{rw}

The domain of feature selection for Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) has been extensively explored through statistical filters, wrapper-based methods, and, more recently, advanced metaheuristic optimization algorithms. This section systematically reviews the existing literature by categorizing it into four primary domains: classical feature selection strategies, swarm intelligence approaches, evolutionary and foraging-inspired techniques, and hybrid deep learning formulations. Following this review, the limitations of current methodologies are identified to contextualize and position the proposed Student Psychology-Based Optimization (SPBO) algorithm within the state-of-the-art.

\subsection{Classical Feature Selection Strategies}

Early work on dimensionality reduction for IDS relied on filter and wrapper
methods. Zaman and Karray \cite{b5} selected features for an SVM-based detector
using a filter ranking, and Revathi and Malathi \cite{b6} analysed the NSL-KDD
dataset under several conventional classifiers to establish which attributes
contribute most to detection. Abdulhammed et al. \cite{b24} compared
dimensionality reduction approaches for network IDS and showed that aggressive
reduction can preserve detection quality while lowering inference cost. Direct
comparisons of the two families \cite{b25} indicate the familiar trade-off:
filters are inexpensive but ignore classifier feedback, whereas wrappers are
accuracy-driven but scale poorly with the number of attributes. A broader
performance analysis of feature subset selection for IDS \cite{b26} reaches the
same conclusion, namely that exhaustive subset search becomes intractable well
before the dimensionality of modern traffic captures is reached. These findings
motivate the use of metaheuristic search, which approximates the optimal subset
without enumerating the search space; however, they also establish that any such
search must be judged on computational cost as well as on accuracy.

\subsection{Swarm Intelligence-Based Feature Selection}

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{b7} is one of the earliest swarm
intelligence algorithms and remains a common starting point for feature
selection in IDS research. Because the cost of its search grows rapidly with the
dimensionality of the input, PSO is frequently preceded by a dimensionality
reduction step such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), so that the swarm
explores a smaller and less redundant space. Zhou et al. \cite{b8} improved the
Harris Hawk Optimization (HHO) algorithm for a hybrid IDS, using it to select a
feature subset that was then classified with K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) for both
binary and multi-level classification; their results indicate that careful
pre-processing can raise detection accuracy while simultaneously reducing
training and testing time. A modified Grey Wolf Optimizer (MGWO) was adopted by
Alzaqebah et al. \cite{b9} to drive the feature selection stage of an IDS, where
alterations to the standard GWO search improved detection accuracy and reached a
solution in fewer iterations, after which a Support Vector Machine (SVM)
performed the classification. Vijayanand et al. \cite{b10} augmented the Whale
Optimization Algorithm (WOA) with genetic operators: because WOA tends to
converge towards locally optimal solutions, mutation and crossover are
introduced before the fitness calculation stage to diversify the search and
retain the most informative feature set, again with an SVM as the classifier.
Alazzam et al. \cite{b11} applied the Pigeon-Inspired Optimizer (PIO)
\cite{b3}, deriving two binary variants, Sigmoid\textunderscore PIO and
Cosine\textunderscore PIO, in which the transfer function is applied at the
fitness evaluation stage; the resulting models converge faster than the
traditional methods against which they were compared. Collectively, these
studies demonstrate that swarm intelligence can identify compact and
discriminative feature subsets. Their update rules are nevertheless contractive,
in the sense that every candidate is drawn towards an incumbent reference
solution, so the population loses diversity as it converges and remains
vulnerable to the local optima that the authors of \cite{b10} explicitly set out
to escape.

\subsection{Evolutionary and Foraging-Based Feature Selection}

A second family of studies draws on evolutionary and foraging behaviour.
Aslahi-Shahri et al. \cite{b4} hybridised a Genetic Algorithm (GA) with an SVM,
using the GA to search the subset space and the SVM to score each candidate. In
\cite{b12}, a distributed IDS is built from a combination of the Ant Colony
Optimization (ACO) algorithm and an SVM classifier, a pairing the authors report
to be particularly effective. A hybrid IDS was proposed in \cite{b13} using the
Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm for feature selection together with a
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), which served as the classifier discriminating
between regular and aberrant packets in the network data, reaching an accuracy
of around 87.54\%. Gotorlar et al. \cite{b14} improved IDS performance by
pairing a normalized Harmony Search (HS) algorithm with an SVM, and contributed
an approach for converting character data into the numerical form that HS
requires. Syarif et al. \cite{b15} proposed a feature selection algorithm for
IDS based on Cuckoo Search (CS), a novel metaheuristic at the time, which
compared favourably with the alternatives then available, and a hybridisation of
an MLP with the Bat algorithm is proposed in \cite{b16}. Across this group the
methodological template is remarkably uniform---a population-based search
narrows the feature space and a conventional classifier makes the final
decision---and the reported gains stem largely from the choice of metaheuristic
rather than from the classifier. What these studies share with the swarm-based
group is the absence of an explicit mechanism for deciding \emph{when} to
explore rather than exploit; exploration is either fixed by the operator
probabilities or left to a purely stochastic phase.

\subsection{Hybrid and Deep Learning-Assisted Approaches}

More recently, metaheuristic search has been combined with deep models rather
than with classical classifiers. Rahman et al. \cite{b21} coupled swarm-based
optimization with an LSTM network for intelligent intrusion detection, showing
that the optimizer remains the decisive component of the pipeline even when the
downstream classifier is a deep sequence model. Such hybrids improve
representational capacity, but they also increase training cost and reduce
transparency, which is a material concern in security operations where an
analyst must be able to justify why traffic was flagged. Interpretability is
largely absent from the IDS feature selection studies surveyed above: the
selected subsets are reported as a list of attribute indices, without evidence
that those attributes are operationally meaningful for distinguishing attack
classes.

\subsection{Student Psychology-Based Optimization}

SPBO was introduced by Das et al. \cite{b17} as a population-based metaheuristic
that models the learning psychology of students in a class, and it has since
been applied to numerical optimization and practical engineering problems
\cite{b20}. Its appeal for feature selection lies in the variable-dependent
categorisation of candidates, which allows one individual to behave as a good
student in one dimension and as an average student in another, thereby
sustaining diversity more effectively than a single global update rule. In our
earlier work \cite{b19} SPBO was applied to intrusion detection feature
selection with explainable AI, which established the viability of the approach
but retained the original update equations, and therefore also retained SPBO's
dependence on the current best student and its purely random improvement phase.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning of This Work}

Taken together, the surveyed literature establishes that metaheuristic feature
selection outperforms exhaustive and filter-based alternatives for IDS, and that
the choice of search dynamics matters more than the choice of classifier.
Three gaps nevertheless remain. First, the dominant algorithms are contractive
and therefore stagnate in local optima once the incumbent solution ceases to
improve, which the hybridisations in \cite{b10,b11} address only partially by
grafting on evolutionary operators. Second, exploration and exploitation are
balanced implicitly through fixed operator probabilities rather than through an
adaptive policy, so the computational budget is spent uniformly regardless of
whether the search is still making progress. Third, interpretability is treated
as outside the scope of feature selection, leaving the security relevance of the
selected attributes unverified.

This work addresses these three gaps simultaneously. An $\epsilon$-greedy policy
replaces the implicit exploration of standard SPBO with an explicit and tunable
probabilistic decision, and a Lévy flight mechanism supplies the heavy-tailed,
long-range jumps that contractive update rules cannot produce, allowing agents
to leave local optima rather than to oscillate around them. SHAP and LIME are
then applied to the selected subset so that the operational relevance of the
retained attributes is demonstrated rather than assumed. The resulting
$\epsilon$-L-SPBO algorithm is evaluated against the state-of-the-art methods
discussed above on the NSL-KDD dataset under a common experimental protocol.

\section{Student Psychology-Based Optimization (SPBO)}
\label{spbo}

Student Psychology-based Optimization algorithm (SPBO) is a new metaheuristic optimization algorithm that is based on the psychology of the students \cite{b17}. In this framework, each individual in the population represents a student, each decision variable corresponds to an academic subject, and the fitness value reflects the student's overall academic performance. During the optimization process, individual search agents continuously update their positions by simulating distinct learning behaviors to converge toward the global optimum. The individual with the highest fitness score is identified as the best student in the class, while the remaining students adjust their performance according to their own learning psychology and the performance gap relative to either the best student or the class average. Based on these psychological characteristics, the population is categorized into four distinct groups: the best student, good students, average students, and students who improve randomly. These four behavioral cohorts define the primary operational phases of the SPBO algorithm, guiding the global search space exploration and local refinement. In this optimization algorithm, students are categorized into four types:

\textbf{Best Student:} The student who obtained the highest overall marks/ grade in the examination is the best student in the class. The BS will always try to maintain her/his position by obtaining the highest marks in class. To obtain the highest marks best student needs to provide more effort than other students. The best student’s improvement may be expressed with the help of equation \eqref{q1}.

\vspace{-15pt}

\begin{align}\label{q1}
X_{\text{bestnew}} = X_{\text{B}} + (-1)^k \times \text{R} \times (X_{\text{B}} - X_j) \tag{1}
\end{align}

where, $X_{B}$ and $X_{j}$ are, respectively, the marks obtained by the best student and randomly selected $j^{th}$ student in a particular subject, $R$ is a random number in between 0 and 1, k is a random parameter either 1 or 2.

\textbf{Good Student:} If a student finds interest in any subject, then s/he makes a good result in that subject. They are good students who perform well in some subjects not in all subjects. It is a random process because everyone's mindset is not the same. Some students work hard as the best students. This category of the student may be represented with the help of equation \eqref{q2}. Some students give more effort than the average student. This category of student may be expressed with the help of equation \eqref{q3}. The equation of these two category of good students are:

\vspace{-15pt}

\begin{align*}\label{q2}
X_{\text{newi}} &= X_{\text{B}} + \text{[R} \times (X_{\text{B}} - X_i)\text{]} \tag{2a} \\\label{q3}
 X_{\text{newi}} &= X_i + \text{[R} \times (X_{\text{B}} - X_i) + \text{[R} \times (X_i - X_{\text{mean}})\text{]}\tag{2b}
\end{align*}

where, $X_i$ is the marks obtained by the $i^{th}$ student in that subject, $X_{mean}$ is the average performance of the class in that particular subject and $R$ is a random number (0 to 1).

\textbf{Average Student:} Since a student's effort depends on how engaged they are in the courses being supplied to them if they are less interested in any of the subjects, they will give that subject an average effort. The performance of this group of students can be modeled by equation \eqref{q4}.

\vspace{-10pt}

\begin{equation}\label{q4}
    X_{\text{newi}} = X_i + \text{R} \times (X_{\text{mean}} - X_i)  \tag{3}
\end{equation}

where, $X_i$ and mean are, in order, the marks obtained by the $i^{th}$  student and the average marks obtained by the class in that specific subject, and $R$ is a random number (0 to 1).

\textbf{Students Who Try to Improve Randomly:} Other than the three groups of pupils listed above, some students attempt to raise their performance on their own. Depending on the subject, they try to put up some effort in a random manner. This group of students tries to put forth effort haphazardly in the topic to boost overall exam results. The use of \eqref{q5} can be used to convey the student's performance in this category.

\vspace{-15pt}

\begin{align*}\label{q5}
    X_{\text{newi}} = X_{\text{min}} + \text{rand} \times (X_{\text{max}} - X_{\text{min}}) \tag{4}
\end{align*}

where, $X_{min}$ and $X_{max}$ are the minimum and the maximum limit of marks of the subject, respectively.

In short, like other population-based optimization algorithms, SPBO begins with a randomly generated population, where the fitness of each candidate solution is evaluated. In each iteration, the best individual is identified, after which the decision variables of the remaining individuals are categorized into four student types according to their learning behaviors. The corresponding update mechanism is then applied to each variable. The resulting solutions undergo boundary checking to keep them within the feasible search space. If an updated solution yields a superior fitness value, it replaces the current solution; otherwise, the original solution is retained. This procedure continues until the specified stopping criterion is met \cite{b20}.

A key feature of SPBO is its variable-dependent and dynamic student categorization. Except for the best student, an individual can be classified differently across dimensions—for example, as a good student in one dimension, an average student in another, and a randomly improving student in a third. This adaptive mechanism promotes search diversity and supports a balance between exploration of the global search space and exploitation of promising regions. The pseudocode of SPBO is given in Figure~\ref{f3}.

\section{The Proposed $\epsilon$-L-SPBO Algorithm}
\label{ms}

Although SPBO has shown promising optimization performance by modeling different student learning behaviors, several weaknesses still remain in its search process. First, the search direction is strongly influenced by the current best student. Once this individual falls into a local optimum, the population may be guided toward a limited search space, resulting in premature convergence. Second, standard SPBO struggles to maintain an effective balance between global exploration and local exploitation throughout the iterative process. The algorithm often performs redundant searches in sub-optimal regions, leading to excessive computational runtime and slow convergence speed when applied to high-dimensional feature selection tasks. Third, although the random-improvement phase aims to maintain population diversity, its purely stochastic updates often fail to efficiently explore unvisited regions or refine promising feature subsets.

To address these limitations, two advanced algorithmic strategies are integrated into the original SPBO framework: an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy and a Lévy flight mechanism. The $\epsilon$-greedy strategy establishes a dynamic probabilistic framework that adaptively balances exploration (discovering new feature subsets) and exploitation (refining elite solutions) throughout the optimization process. Concurrently, the inclusion of Lévy flight—a random walk governed by a heavy-tailed step-length distribution—introduces long-range exploratory jumps, enabling search agents to escape local optima stagnation and rapidly locate promising global optima. As a result, the proposed modified SPBO accelerates convergence, reduces computational runtime, and improves classification performance for intrusion detection systems.

The remainder of this section formalises the proposed algorithm: Section~\ref{sec:fitness} defines the fitness function, Section~\ref{sec:enhanced-spbo} derives the $\epsilon$-greedy and L\'evy flight update rules and analyses their complexity, and Section~\ref{sec:binary-conversion} describes the binary conversion that turns the continuous search into feature selection.

\subsection{Fitness Function}
\label{sec:fitness}

Fitness functions are the terminology for a procedure to evaluate the fitness of the solutions. The fitness function evaluates the solution which is a subset of selected features in terms of True Positive Rate (TPR), False Positive Rate (FPR), and number of features. The formula \eqref{q6} used to evaluate the student or solution fitness is:

\begin{equation*}\label{q6}
    FF = \sum_{i=1}^{N} i X_i^2  \tag{5}
\end{equation*}

Where FF means Fitness Function,
N is the total number of students, and $X_i$ is the subject-wise performance of the $i^{th}$ student. The used fitness function is a standard benchmark function named \textit{Sum Square}.

\subsection{Enhanced SPBO with $\epsilon$-Greedy Exploration and L\'evy Flight}
\label{sec:enhanced-spbo}

In the original SPBO, every student updates its position through a predefined
learning behaviour that is driven either by the best student or by the mean of
its own group. These behaviours exploit promising regions of the search space
efficiently, but they are all contractive: each candidate is pulled towards a
reference point that already belongs to the current population. Consequently,
the population progressively loses diversity and, for high-dimensional feature
selection problems, the search is prone to stagnate around a local optimum
before the iteration budget is exhausted.

To mitigate this limitation while preserving the learning behaviour of the
original algorithm, two complementary mechanisms are introduced into the
average-student and the random-improvement update rules: (i) an
$\epsilon$-greedy policy that decides, for every decision variable
independently, whether the variable is updated by exploitation or by
exploration; and (ii) a L\'evy flight operator that performs occasional
long-range jumps when exploration is selected. The best-student and
good-student behaviours, given by Equations~(1)--(2b), remain unchanged, so the
convergence characteristics of SPBO are retained.

\subsubsection{L\'evy Flight Operator}

A L\'evy flight is a random walk whose step lengths are drawn from a
heavy-tailed L\'evy distribution, $L(s)\sim|s|^{-1-\beta}$ with
$0<\beta\leq 2$. Most of the generated steps are small, which refines the
current region, whereas a small fraction of the steps are very large, which
allows the search to reach regions that cannot be attained by the contractive
SPBO operators. The steps are generated with Mantegna's algorithm
\cite{ref-mantegna}, in which a L\'evy-distributed step $s$ is obtained from
two normally distributed variables $u$ and $v$:

\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{u}{|v|^{1/\beta}}, \qquad
    u \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\sigma_u^{2}\right), \qquad
    v \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,1\right),
    \tag{6} \label{eq:levy-step}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the scale parameter $\sigma_u$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_u =
    \left[
        \frac{\Gamma(1+\beta)\,\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\pi\beta}{2}\right)}
             {\Gamma\!\left(\dfrac{1+\beta}{2}\right)\,\beta\,2^{(\beta-1)/2}}
    \right]^{1/\beta},
    \tag{7} \label{eq:levy-sigma}
\end{equation}

\noindent and $\Gamma(\cdot)$ denotes the gamma function. The shape parameter is
set to $\beta=1.5$, which is the value most commonly adopted for L\'evy-based
metaheuristics. A single scalar step is drawn for each decision variable that is
selected for exploration, and the corresponding long jump is applied
additively:

\begin{equation}
    X_{i,j}^{\,t+1} =
    \mathrm{clip}\!\left(X_{i,j}^{\,t} + A\,s,\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
    \tag{8} \label{eq:levy-move}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $X_{i,j}^{\,t}$ is the $j$-th variable of student $i$ at
iteration $t$, $A$ is the amplitude that scales the L\'evy step, $LB_j$ and
$UB_j$ are the lower and upper bounds of the $j$-th variable, and
$\mathrm{clip}(\cdot)$ is the boundary-control operator that keeps the candidate
inside the feasible search space.

The second exploration operator is a uniform spread over the region currently
occupied by the population, which relocates the variable without any bias
towards the best or the mean student:

\begin{equation}
    X_{i,j}^{\,t+1} =
    \mathrm{clip}\!\left(m_j + r_1\left(M_j - m_j\right),\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
    \tag{9} \label{eq:uniform-spread}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $m_j=\min_{k} X_{k,j}^{\,t}$ and $M_j=\max_{k} X_{k,j}^{\,t}$ are
the minimum and the maximum values of the $j$-th variable over the whole
population, and $r_1\sim U(0,1)$.

The characteristic behaviour of the operator---many short steps interspersed
with rare long jumps---is illustrated in Figure~\ref{f4}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_s4_levy_steps.pdf}
    \caption{L\'evy flight step lengths: the heavy-tailed distribution produces predominantly local refinement with occasional long jumps that escape local optima.}
    \label{f4}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{$\epsilon$-Greedy Update Rules}

The $\epsilon$-greedy policy \cite{ref-sutton} controls when the exploration
operators of Equations~\eqref{eq:levy-move} and \eqref{eq:uniform-spread} are
activated. For every student of the two affected groups and for every decision
variable, a random number $\rho\sim U(0,1)$ is drawn. If $\rho<\epsilon$ the
variable is updated by exploration; otherwise the original SPBO behaviour is
applied. When exploration is selected, a second random number
$\rho_L\sim U(0,1)$ decides between the L\'evy jump and the uniform spread
through the threshold $P_{\mathrm{threshold}}$. Because the decision is taken
per variable rather than per student, a single candidate solution can combine
exploited and explored dimensions, which preserves the useful part of a good
solution while perturbing the remainder.

For a student $i$ belonging to the average-student group $\mathcal{A}$, the
update rule of Equation~(3) is replaced by

\begin{equation}
    X_{i,j}^{\,t+1} =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathrm{clip}\!\left(X_{i,j}^{\,t} + A\,s,\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
            & \rho < \epsilon \;\wedge\; \rho_L \geq P_{\mathrm{threshold}},\\[6pt]
        \mathrm{clip}\!\left(m_j + r_1\left(M_j-m_j\right),\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
            & \rho < \epsilon \;\wedge\; \rho_L < P_{\mathrm{threshold}},\\[6pt]
        \mathrm{clip}\!\left(X_{i,j}^{\,t}
            + r_1\left(\mu_j^{\mathcal{A}} - X_{i,j}^{\,t}\right),\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
            & \rho \geq \epsilon,
    \end{cases}
    \tag{10} \label{eq:avg-eps}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu_j^{\mathcal{A}}$ is the mean value of the $j$-th variable
over the average-student group. The third case is exactly the original
average-student behaviour, so the enhanced rule degenerates to standard SPBO
when $\epsilon=0$.

Similarly, for a student $i$ belonging to the group $\mathcal{R}$ of students
who try to improve randomly, the update rule of Equation~(4) becomes

\begin{equation}
    X_{i,j}^{\,t+1} =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathrm{clip}\!\left(X_{i,j}^{\,t} + A\,s,\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
            & \rho < \epsilon \;\wedge\; \rho_L \geq P_{\mathrm{threshold}},\\[6pt]
        \mathrm{clip}\!\left(m_j + r_1\left(M_j-m_j\right),\; LB_j,\; UB_j\right),
            & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \tag{11} \label{eq:rnd-eps}
\end{equation}

In this group the exploitation branch is itself a uniform random relocation,
which is the original behaviour of Equation~(4); the enhancement therefore adds
only the possibility of a heavy-tailed jump, and the group keeps its role as the
diversification component of SPBO. The parameters that govern the two
mechanisms are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:eps-levy-params}, and the complete
procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:enhanced-spbo}.

        \toprule
        \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Description} \\
        \midrule

            Probability that a decision variable is updated by exploration

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Enhanced SPBO with $\epsilon$-greedy exploration and L\'evy flight.}
\label{alg:enhanced-spbo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Objective function $f(\cdot)$, population size $N$, dimension $D$,
         bounds $LB$, $UB$, maximum iterations $T_{\max}$,
         $\epsilon$, $\beta$, $A$, $P_{\mathrm{threshold}}$
\Ensure  Best solution $X_{\mathrm{best}}$, best fitness $F_{\mathrm{best}}$
\State Initialise the population $X=\{X_1,X_2,\dots,X_N\}$ randomly within $[LB,UB]$
\State Evaluate $f(X_i)$ for $i=1,2,\dots,N$ and determine $X_{\mathrm{best}}$, $F_{\mathrm{best}}$
\For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $T_{\max}$}
    \State Classify the population into best, good, average and random-improvement students
    \For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
        \For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $D$}
            \If{$X_i$ is the best student}
                \State Update $X_{i,j}$ by Equation~(1)
            \ElsIf{$X_i$ is a good student}
                \State Update $X_{i,j}$ by Equation~(2a) or Equation~(2b)
            \ElsIf{$X_i$ is an average student}
                \State Update $X_{i,j}$ by Equation~\eqref{eq:avg-eps}
                       \Comment{$\epsilon$-greedy}
            \Else
                \State Update $X_{i,j}$ by Equation~\eqref{eq:rnd-eps}
                       \Comment{$\epsilon$-greedy}
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State Apply boundary control to $X_i^{\mathrm{new}}$ and evaluate $f(X_i^{\mathrm{new}})$
        \If{$f(X_i^{\mathrm{new}})$ is better than $f(X_i)$}
            \State $X_i \gets X_i^{\mathrm{new}}$
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Update $X_{\mathrm{best}}$ and $F_{\mathrm{best}}$
\EndFor
\State \Return $X_{\mathrm{best}}$, $F_{\mathrm{best}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

The cost of the proposed algorithm follows directly from Algorithm~\ref{alg:enhanced-spbo}.
Let $N$ be the population size, $D$ the number of decision variables, $T_{\max}$ the
iteration budget, and $C_f$ the cost of one fitness evaluation. Each iteration
classifies the population into the four behavioural groups, which requires
sorting the fitness values at $O(N\log N)$; updates every decision variable of
every student, which requires $O(ND)$; and evaluates one candidate per student,
which requires $O(NC_f)$. The overall complexity is therefore

\begin{equation}
    O\!\left(T_{\max}\,N\left(\log N + D + C_f\right)\right),
    \tag{12} \label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is the same order as the original SPBO. The $\epsilon$-greedy
decision adds one uniform random draw per variable and the L\'evy operator adds
two normal draws and a power operation per explored variable, so the additional
work is $O(ND)$ per iteration and does not change the asymptotic cost. Because
only a fraction $\epsilon$ of the variables invoke the L\'evy operator, the
expected additional cost per iteration is $O(\epsilon ND)$. For feature
selection $C_f$ dominates, since each evaluation trains and scores a classifier
on the candidate subset; the practical runtime reduction reported in
Section~\ref{eara} therefore arises from reaching a satisfactory subset in fewer
iterations rather than from a lower per-iteration cost.

The decision process followed at each update is illustrated in Figure~\ref{f_eps}.

\subsection{Binary Conversion and Feature Selection}
\label{sec:binary-conversion}

After calculating the fitness, a fixed threshold value $T_h$ is selected to
determine which features are more relevant. If the best fitness value of a
particular feature, that is of a student $X$, crosses the selected threshold
value, its binary conversion value $X_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is set to 1; otherwise it
is set to 0. Formally, the selection criterion is given by
Equations~\eqref{eq:bin-one} and \eqref{eq:bin-zero}:

\begin{align}
    X_{\mathrm{bestFitness}} &\geq T_h & &\rightarrow & X_{\mathrm{bin}} &= 1,
        \tag{13} \label{eq:bin-one} \\
    X_{\mathrm{bestFitness}} &< T_h & &\rightarrow & X_{\mathrm{bin}} &= 0,
        \tag{14} \label{eq:bin-zero}
\end{align}

\noindent where $X_{\mathrm{bestFitness}}$ is the best fitness value of a
student, $T_h$ is the selected threshold value, which is generally assumed to be
0.5, and $X_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is the binary conversion value of a student, which
is either 0 or 1.

After the binary conversion, a feature is selected if its converted value is 1
and rejected otherwise. In this way the continuous optimisation algorithm is
transformed into its binary counterpart, and feature selection is performed
through this binary conversion technique, as illustrated in
Table~\ref{tab:binary-representation}.

\subsection{Data Pre-processing}
\label{sec:preproc}

\textbf{Data Cleaning:} Missing values, outliers and inconsistencies are resolved first. Because a large number of duplicate records can bias a classifier towards the repeated patterns, duplicate records are removed, after which any remaining null or missing value is replaced by a random value drawn from the observed range of the corresponding column.

\textbf{Data Transformation:} NSL-KDD records several attributes, such as the protocol type, the service and the connection flag, as categorical text rather than as numbers. Because the classifiers used in this study operate on numerical input, these attributes are converted with one-hot encoding.

\textbf{Data Normalization:} Data scaling refers to scaling the data into a specific range. If the data values fall outside the intended range (e.g., values are too large or too small), it can cause several issues during training or testing. It also can affect the performance of ML models, since the choice of input scaling method has a measurable impact on classifier prediction accuracy \cite{b2}. A standard range is established for every column; values falling below or above that range are clipped to the lower and upper bound, respectively, after which each attribute is rescaled to the unit interval. Equation \eqref{q9} shows the normalization procedure of the value.

\vspace{-6pt}

\[\label{q9}
X_{\text{normalized}} = \frac{{X - X_{\text{min}}}}{{X_{\text{max}} - X_{\text{min}}}} \tag{15}
\]

The complete sequence of pre-processing operations is shown in Figure~\ref{f5}.

\begin{figure}[H]

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The selected subsets are assessed with the four standard classification metrics.
Let $TP$, $TN$, $FP$ and $FN$ denote the number of true positives, true
negatives, false positives and false negatives, respectively. Accuracy measures
the overall proportion of correct decisions:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN},
    \tag{16} \label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}

\noindent precision measures the proportion of traffic flagged as an attack that
is genuinely an attack, and therefore reflects the false alarm burden placed on
an analyst:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP},
    \tag{17} \label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

\noindent recall, equivalently the true positive rate, measures the proportion of
actual attacks that are detected:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN},
    \tag{18} \label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}

\noindent and the F1-score is their harmonic mean, which summarises the two in a
single value and is the more informative statistic on an imbalanced dataset such
as NSL-KDD:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{F1} = 2 \cdot
        \frac{\mathrm{Precision} \cdot \mathrm{Recall}}
             {\mathrm{Precision} + \mathrm{Recall}}.
    \tag{19} \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}

\noindent Because the class distribution reported in Table~\ref{tab:dataset} is
highly skewed, the metrics are additionally reported as weighted averages over
the classes, each class being weighted by its support.

\subsection{Feature Selection}

The NSL-KDD dataset contains 41 features, not all features are important to build IDS. A subset of those features must be selected to achieve a high detection rate and low false alarms. Moreover, the feature selection process is important to eliminate the number of features that are unnecessary to build an efficient IDS.

\subsection{Classifier Training and Testing}

Once the feature selection process is accomplished using the proposed method, the selected subset of features is used to train several machine learning classifiers, which aim to distinguish between normal and attack classes. Each trained model is then evaluated on a held-out testing set that is disjoint from the data used for selection and training. A feature subset is therefore judged by the detection performance it yields on unseen traffic rather than by its size alone.

\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{abl}

The results of Section~\ref{eara} establish that $\epsilon$-L-SPBO performs
well, but they do not attribute that performance to either of the two
mechanisms introduced in Section~\ref{sec:enhanced-spbo}. This section
separates their contributions and examines the sensitivity of the algorithm to
its three parameters. Four families of experiments are reported: a component
ablation that disables each mechanism in turn, and sensitivity analyses for the
exploration probability $\epsilon$, the L\'evy shape parameter $\beta$, and the
exploration threshold $P_{\mathrm{threshold}}$.

The ablation exploits the fact that the enhanced update rules degenerate
to the original ones at particular parameter values. Setting $\epsilon = 0$
prevents the exploration branch of Equations~\eqref{eq:avg-eps}
and~\eqref{eq:rnd-eps} from ever firing, which recovers standard SPBO; setting
$P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 1$ allows exploration but never selects the L\'evy
jump, isolating the $\epsilon$-greedy policy; and setting
$P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 0$ routes every exploration step through the L\'evy
operator. Each configuration was run with five independent seeds
(42, 52, 62, 72, 82) under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, and results
are reported as mean~$\pm$~SD so that they are directly comparable with
Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.

\subsection{Component Ablation}
\label{sec:component-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-component} reports the effect of disabling each
mechanism. The full algorithm is the best configuration on every column. It
attains 99.91~$\pm$~0.04\% accuracy against 99.81~$\pm$~0.09\% for the
unmodified baseline, retains 10.0~$\pm$~0.7 features against 13.2~$\pm$~1.6,
and reaches its best fitness after 7.2~$\pm$~0.8 iterations against
15.2~$\pm$~2.4. These values reproduce the $\epsilon$-L-SPBO and SPBO rows of
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} to within one standard deviation, so the ablation and
the main comparison describe the same behaviour.

Each mechanism contributes on its own, but neither accounts for the full gain.
The $\epsilon$-greedy policy alone removes 1.4 features and 3.8 iterations
relative to the baseline while lifting accuracy by 0.04 points: injecting
stochastic deviations into the average- and random-student updates is enough to
escape the early stagnation that fixes the baseline on a larger subset. The
L\'evy operator alone produces a similar mean improvement but with the widest
dispersion of the four configurations (0.11 points in accuracy, 3.7 iterations
in convergence), which is consistent with the heavy-tailed step distribution:
when every exploration step is a long jump, progress depends strongly on whether
an early jump happens to land in a productive region. Combining the two
recovers more than the sum of the individual effects --- a further 0.06
accuracy points and 4.2 iterations beyond the better single mechanism --- and
simultaneously gives the tightest standard deviation in every column. The
$\epsilon$-greedy policy supplies frequent local perturbations that refine a
promising subset, while the fraction $1-P_{\mathrm{threshold}}$ of steps routed
through the L\'evy operator supplies the occasional long jump needed to leave a
basin; used together they are both faster and more reliable than either used
alone, which supports the design choice of Section~\ref{sec:enhanced-spbo}.
The fitness column shows the same ordering: the full algorithm reaches the
lowest objective value, 0.0152~$\pm$~0.0006, reflecting the higher accuracy on
the smaller subset that the fitness function of Section~\ref{sec:fitness}
rewards.

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-sensitivity} and Figure~\ref{f_abl} report the
sensitivity of the algorithm to its three parameters. The configuration adopted
in the main experiments, $\epsilon = 0.10$, $\beta = 1.5$ and
$P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 0.6$, is the best entry of every sweep, and the
surface around it is flat enough that the algorithm does not require careful
tuning to reproduce it.

The sweep over $\epsilon$ is the sharpest of the three. Disabling exploration
($\epsilon = 0$) returns the baseline, with 13.2 features and 15.2 iterations;
raising $\epsilon$ to 0.10 removes three features and halves the iteration
count. Beyond that point the trend reverses: at $\epsilon = 0.30$ almost a third
of updates ignore the learned direction, the search behaves increasingly like a
random walk, and both the subset size and the dispersion grow again. The
interior optimum at $\epsilon = 0.10$ is the expected signature of an
exploration--exploitation trade-off.

The sweep over $\beta$ is comparatively flat, with accuracy spanning 0.05
percentage points and a shallow optimum at $\beta = 1.5$. This is explained by
the rate at which the operator is invoked rather than by the operator itself: at
$\epsilon = 0.1$ and $P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 0.6$ only about 4\% of decision
variables are updated by a L\'evy jump, so the shape of the step distribution
has limited opportunity to influence the binary conversion of
Section~\ref{sec:binary-conversion}. The algorithm is therefore robust to the
choice of $\beta$ within the range examined, and $\beta = 1.5$ can be retained
as the default without tuning.

The sweep over $P_{\mathrm{threshold}}$ confirms the conclusion of the component
ablation from the opposite direction. Routing every exploration step through the
L\'evy operator ($P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 0$) is the worst and most variable
setting, and suppressing the operator entirely ($P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 1$)
recovers the $\epsilon$-greedy-only configuration; the best results are obtained
at the intermediate value 0.6, where roughly two in five exploration steps are
long jumps.

\subsection{Interpretation and Limitations}
\label{sec:ablation-limits}

Three conclusions follow. First, the advantage of the proposed algorithm is
genuinely joint. Neither the $\epsilon$-greedy policy nor the L\'evy operator
reproduces the full result on its own; each recovers roughly half of the
reduction in subset size and iteration count, and only their combination
reaches 99.91\% accuracy on 10 features in 7 iterations. The two mechanisms act
on different scales --- frequent small perturbations against rare long jumps ---
which is why they compose rather than merely duplicate one another.

Second, the gain is largest on cost and stability, and smallest on accuracy.
The full algorithm halves the iteration count and removes a quarter of the
retained features relative to the baseline, but improves accuracy by only 0.10
percentage points, because the baseline already operates near the ceiling that
NSL-KDD permits for a Random Forest classifier. This matches the complexity
analysis of Section~\ref{sec:complexity}, which attributes the runtime advantage
to requiring fewer fitness evaluations rather than to a cheaper iteration. It
also means the accuracy column should be read alongside the standard
deviations: the 0.10-point margin over the baseline is roughly one combined
standard deviation, so it is a consistent rather than a decisive separation,
whereas the margins on features and iterations are several standard deviations
wide and are established firmly.

Third, these results are consistent with Section~\ref{sec:comparison}: the rows
for $\epsilon$-L-SPBO and SPBO in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} fall within one
standard deviation of the corresponding rows here. So, the single-seed main
comparison is representative rather than a fortunate draw. The remaining
limitation is that the competing metaheuristics in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}
were not themselves re-run across seeds; extending the multi-seed protocol of
this section to those baselines would place the whole comparison on the same
evidential footing.

\section{Discussion}
\label{di}

This study asked whether the two weaknesses of Student Psychology-Based Optimization identified in Section~\ref{ms}---its dependence on the current best student and its implicit handling of exploration---can be addressed without discarding the learning behaviour that makes the algorithm effective. The evidence assembled in Sections~\ref{eara} and~\ref{abl} answers that question affirmatively, but it also places the contribution more narrowly than accuracy alone would suggest: the measurable benefit of the enhancement lies in how quickly and how reliably a compact subset is reached, not in the accuracy eventually attained.

\subsection{Principal Findings}
\label{sec:principal-findings}

The primary objective of this study was to resolve the key bottlenecks in machine learning-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)—namely, the premature local convergence in feature selection algorithms and excessive computational runtime. The experimental evaluation on the widely benchmarked NSL-KDD dataset confirms that our proposed modified Student Psychology-Based Optimization framework, incorporating an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy and Lévy flight ($\epsilon$-L-SPBO), successfully overcomes these challenges.

\subsection{Why the Enhancement Works}
\label{sec:why}

A critical requirement for deployable IDS in real-time network environments is feature selection. Standard continuous metaheuristic algorithms often perform redundant search operations and suffer from search space stagnation. Feature selection is inherently a discrete binary optimization problem; therefore, applying a continuous swarm intelligence algorithm requires an effective transfer function and a discretized search dynamic. By introducing the $\epsilon$-greedy mechanism, the proposed algorithm establishes a dynamic probabilistic trade-off between global exploration and local exploitation. This prevents search agents from greedily clustering around sub-optimal feature combinations early in the optimization process. Currently, the Lévy flight mechanism—characterized by heavy-tailed step-length distributions—enables long-range exploratory jumps. This allows search agents trapped in local minima (a known limitation of the standard SPBO's best-student dependence) to break free, resulting in substantially faster convergence and drastically reduced algorithm execution runtime compared to classical metaheuristic approaches.

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Detection Performance}
\label{sec:disc-reduction}

As a direct result of these strategic enhancements, the proposed $\epsilon$-L-SPBO algorithm reduced the NSL-KDD dataset from 41 original network features to an optimal subset of just 10 key features. Despite eliminating over 75\% of the feature space, the multi-level Random Forest (RF) classifier trained on this minimal 10-feature subset achieved a top-tier classification accuracy of 99.91\%, alongside superior True Positive Rates (TPR) and minimal false alarm rates. This confirms that the 31 removed features contained significant noise, redundancy, and uninformative network traffic attributes that previously degraded model execution speed and accuracy.

\subsection{Interpretability of the Selected Subset}
\label{sec:disc-xai}

Furthermore, to address the transparency concerns surrounding automated network defense decisions, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques—specifically SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME)—were integrated into the framework. XAI interpretations confirmed that the 10 features selected by $\epsilon$-L-SPBO carry the highest operational importance to distinguish normal network traffic from various classes of cyber attacks. This indicates that the optimization process not only improves numerical performance but also preserves domain-relevant security semantics.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing IDS Feature Selection Studies}
\label{sec:disc-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:related-work} positions the proposed algorithm against the
metaheuristic feature selection studies reviewed in Section~\ref{rw}. Two
observations follow. First, the methodological template of that literature---a
population-based search narrows the attribute space and a conventional
classifier makes the final decision---is retained here; the contribution lies in
the search dynamics rather than in the pipeline. Second, the accuracy figures
reported by those studies are not directly comparable with one another or with
the present work, because the datasets, attack taxonomies, train/test splits and
classifier configurations differ between them. This is why the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} re-runs the competing optimisers under a common
protocol rather than quoting published numbers, and why Table~\ref{tab:related-work}
compares methodological limitations rather than headline accuracies.

\subsection{Why the Proposed Design Is Preferable to Existing Alternatives}
\label{sec:why-better}

The comparison of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} and the ablation of
Section~\ref{abl} support a stronger claim than ``competitive accuracy''. They
indicate that the specific design decisions taken here are better suited to
intrusion detection feature selection than the alternatives adopted in the
literature of Section~\ref{rw}, for five reasons.

\textbf{Explicit exploration is preferable to implicit exploration.} In PSO,
GWO, WOA, HHO and standard SPBO, the willingness of an agent to deviate from
the incumbent solution is an emergent property of the update equation: it decays
as the population contracts and cannot be set independently of the convergence
schedule. The $\epsilon$-greedy policy of
Section~\ref{sec:enhanced-spbo} makes that willingness a first-class,
inspectable parameter. The practical consequence is visible in the $\epsilon$
sweep of Table~\ref{tab:ablation-sensitivity}: moving from $\epsilon = 0$, which
is exactly the implicit regime of the baseline, to $\epsilon = 0.10$ removes
three attributes and halves the iteration count, and the degradation beyond
$\epsilon = 0.20$ shows that the optimum is interior. An algorithm whose
exploration rate is implicit cannot be placed at that optimum, because the
quantity is not exposed; the hybridisations of \cite{b10,b11} illustrate the
point, since grafting crossover or a fixed transfer function onto a contractive
optimiser changes the mechanism of deviation without making its rate adaptive.

\textbf{Two exploration scales are preferable to one.} The component ablation
of Table~\ref{tab:ablation-component} is the decisive evidence here. Each
mechanism used alone recovers roughly half of the improvement---$\epsilon$-greedy
alone reaches 11.8 features in 11.4 iterations, L\'evy alone 12.4 features in
12.6 iterations---while their combination reaches 10.0 features in 7.2
iterations, more than the sum of the parts. The reason is that the two operate
on different scales and therefore address different failure modes: frequent
small perturbations refine a subset within its current basin, whereas rare
heavy-tailed jumps relocate the search between basins. Methods that supply only
one scale pay for this. L\'evy-only search is not merely worse in the mean but
the most variable configuration tested (0.11 points in accuracy, 3.7 iterations
in convergence), since progress then depends on whether an early long jump
happens to land productively. Approaches built on a single diversification
device---pheromone reinforcement in \cite{b12}, static mutation in \cite{b4},
echolocation hybridisation in \cite{b16}---inherit exactly this
single-scale fragility.

\textbf{Convergence cost is the discriminating criterion, and it is where the
margin is large.} The six optimisers in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} span only
0.20 accuracy points, a range comparable to the seed-to-seed dispersion reported
in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-component}; accuracy therefore cannot separate modern
metaheuristics on this benchmark, and published accuracy comparisons that ignore
this are reporting noise. The costs of reaching that accuracy are separated by
several standard deviations and are established firmly: 7 iterations against 15
for SPBO, 17 for PSO, 20 for WOA, 25 for GWO and 30 for HHO. Since the
wrapper fitness dominates the runtime of Section~\ref{sec:complexity}, an
iteration count reduced by half to four fifths translates directly into
training cost, which is the quantity that governs how often an operational
detector can be retrained as traffic drifts. The proposed design is preferable
on the axis where the differences are real, not on the one where they are not.

\textbf{A smaller subset is a stronger result than an equal-accuracy larger
one.} $\epsilon$-L-SPBO retains 10 of 41 attributes, against 13 for SPBO and
15--19 for the swarm baselines, at equal or better accuracy. Fewer retained
attributes mean fewer traffic statistics to instrument at the sensor, lower
per-packet feature extraction latency, and a smaller surface over which an
adversary can manipulate inputs. The parsimony is also what makes the
interpretability analysis tractable: SHAP and LIME over 10 attributes yield an
explanation a security analyst can audit, whereas the same analysis over the 19
attributes retained by HHO would not. Studies that treat subset size as a
by-product rather than as an objective, including the deep-learning-assisted
pipeline of \cite{b21}, forgo this benefit.

\textbf{Robust defaults are preferable to tuned ones.} The sweeps of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation-sensitivity} show a flat response to $\beta$---0.05
accuracy points across the range---and a shallow basin around
$P_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 0.6$, so the configuration of
Table~\ref{tab:eps-levy-params} was fixed once and reused rather than tuned per
dataset. Only $\epsilon$ requires a deliberate choice, and its optimum is a
single interior value with a wide surrounding plateau. A method whose reported
advantage depends on per-dataset tuning transfers poorly to deployment, where no
labelled tuning set for the next network is available; the present design
reaches its reported result from defaults.

Taken together, these points locate the contribution precisely. The pipeline is
deliberately conventional---a population-based search followed by a conventional
classifier---so the comparison isolates the search dynamics, and under a common
re-run protocol those dynamics are faster, more parsimonious and more stable
than the alternatives, while the SHAP and LIME analysis demonstrates rather than
assumes that the retained attributes carry security meaning.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{co}
In this paper, $\epsilon$-L-SPBO, an enhanced bio-inspired feature selection algorithm based on Student Psychology-Based Optimization and coupled with Explainable AI, is proposed for robust network intrusion detection. The method was evaluated on the NSL-KDD dataset against several state-of-the-art metaheuristic feature selection algorithms, and the resulting feature subset was examined with SHAP and LIME.

The principal findings and contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Significant Dimensionality Reduction:} The proposed $\epsilon$-L-SPBO algorithm successfully reduced the feature dimension of the NSL-KDD dataset from 41 features down to a lean subset of only 10 essential features.
    \item \textbf{High Detection Accuracy:} When combined with a Random Forest classifier, the 10-feature model achieved a classification accuracy of 99.91\%, the highest among the metaheuristic feature selection algorithms re-run under an identical protocol, although the margin over the competing methods falls within the seed-to-seed variability quantified in Section~\ref{abl}.
    \item \textbf{Faster and More Stable Convergence:} The proposed algorithm converged in 7 iterations against 15 for the unmodified SPBO and up to 30 for the competing optimisers. The ablation of Section~\ref{abl} attributes this specifically to the combination of the two mechanisms: used alone, the Lévy operator was both the slowest and the most variable configuration, whereas the combined policy reached its best fitness in the fewest and least variable number of iterations.
    \item \textbf{Isolation of the Contribution:} A component ablation over five seeds showed that the benefit of the enhancement is concentrated in convergence behaviour rather than in final accuracy, since all configurations---including the unmodified baseline---converged to subsets of comparable quality.
    \item \textbf{Model Interpretability:} The application of SHAP and LIME provided transparent, feature-level decision explanations, instilling confidence in the model's threat detection capability.
\end{itemize}

Despite these promising results, several avenues remain for future investigation. First, the evaluation should be extended beyond NSL-KDD to contemporary intrusion detection corpora such as CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and to encrypted and IoT traffic, so that the generalisation of the selected subsets can be assessed rather than assumed. Second, the main comparison should be repeated over multiple seeds with reported dispersion and paired significance testing, which would determine whether the accuracy advantage observed here is real or an artefact of run-to-run variability. Third, theoretical convergence proofs would strengthen the mathematical foundation of the modified SPBO framework, complementing the empirical sensitivity analysis already reported. Fourth, future research will explore extending $\epsilon$-L-SPBO to dynamic and streaming network environments characterized by continuous data drift and novel zero-day attacks. Fifth, the hybrid optimization framework can be integrated with reinforcement learning or adaptive parameter control to further refine search efficiency. Finally, the proposed algorithm can be applied to other real-world engineering and cybersecurity domain problems, such as IoT security, microgrid fault diagnosis, UAV path planning, and wireless sensor network optimization, to evaluate its broader generalization ability.
